# Workflow, phase by phase

## Phase 1: Frame the engagement

Before anything, establish:

- **Which side is the client.** This is the single most important fact. The
  contract itself can read as balanced, but the questions document and the
  internal audit are written from one client's interest and risk. Ask if it is
  not stated.
- **What deliverables are wanted.** The full agreement only, or also the client
  questions document, the assumptions list, the open issues list, the internal
  audit, and a deal infographic.
- **Status of the document.** Is it meant to become the binding signature ready
  agreement, or a preliminary framework followed by a fuller definitive
  agreement. This changes how much must be nailed down now.
- **Language and format.** Default to Hebrew and to a Word document that is
  right aligned. Confirm the no dashes rule applies.

Two stage prompting works well for editorial or ideological legal writing:
first establish voice, style and framing; then supply factual material
separately. For pure contract drafting, phase 1 is the equivalent framing step.

## Phase 2: Intake

Read every source document fully before writing. Sources typically include a
memorandum of understanding, an updated draft, the parties' answers to prior
questions, professional documents (appraisal, land registry extract, planning
authority summaries, architect letters), and any letters of intent.

Build an internal map of: parties and their capacity; the property or subject
matter; the commercial mechanics (consideration, investment, profit split,
adjustment and dilution mechanisms, waterfalls); obligations and rights;
timelines and milestones; third parties; tax; and risks. Note every
contradiction between sources and every piece of missing information. Do not
resolve contradictions silently; record them.

When a source is an OSINT or public records check on a counterparty for
legitimate due diligence, report findings factually with the source, and flag
what still needs verification at the registrar of companies or land registry.

## Phase 3: Clarify

Ask the clarifying questions that genuinely change the drafting. Group them:
first, critical items that alter the structure or the economics (for example
who bears a large payment, the exact mechanics of a waterfall, the client's
side); then fillable blanks (dates, cure periods, a penalty sum). Do not ask
for things a forthcoming document will answer. Answer what you can from the
material first, then ask only what remains. One focused round is better than a
long interrogation.

If a money mechanism is intricate (a waterfall, a dilution formula), restate
your interpretation with a short numeric example and confirm it before drafting
thirty pages around it. Getting the economic core wrong invalidates everything.

## Phase 4: Optional client questions document

If the task is to gather missing information from the client, produce the five
column table described in `questions-doc.md`, exhaustive enough that an
experienced lawyer could finalize the contract from the answers without another
round.

## Phase 5: Draft the agreement

Use the section checklist in `contract-sections.md`. Write full contractual
prose, confident and declarative. You may add standard legal mechanisms
(notices, interpretation, severability, force majeure, assignment, dispute
resolution) even if absent from the draft, as long as they do not contradict
the material. Do not shorten the agreement for convenience and do not omit a
needed mechanism merely because it is not explicit in the draft. Where a figure
or decision is genuinely missing, leave a clearly marked blank rather than
inventing one.

## Phase 6: Three self review rounds

Run three independent passes and fix every issue found.

**Round 1, legal completeness.** Check for missing sections and mechanisms
against the checklist. Does every topic that belongs in this deal appear.

**Round 2, commercial risk.** From the client's side, check financing risk,
planning risk, execution risk, tax risk (betterment levy can be enormous), and
third party risk. Make sure the allocation of each risk is deliberate and
reflected in the text.

**Round 3, enforcement and contradictions.** Check internal contradictions,
dates, sums, percentages, cross references to section numbers, and mechanisms
that cannot actually be applied. Verify that a defined trigger (for example a
dilution event) is drafted as the event itself when the magnitude is uncertain,
rather than a brittle numeric threshold that might not fire.

## Phase 7: Four part deliverable

- **Part A**, the full agreement, counterparty facing.
- **Part B**, the assumptions made during drafting.
- **Part C**, open issues that need a human decision before signature.
- **Part D**, the internal legal audit: weaknesses, risks, and the specific
  points the other side's lawyer will attack or demand to change, each tied to
  a clause, written from the client's perspective.

Deliver Part A as its own file. Deliver B, C and D as a separate internal memo,
because internal reasoning must never be sent to the other side. Note in the
chat where each open blank sits so the client can close it.

## Phase 8: Final self test

Ask: would a senior real estate lawyer who received this consider it
professional work fit to pass to the other side for advanced negotiation and
signature. If the answer is not a full yes, keep fixing until it is. Frame the
known exposure points as expected negotiation positions, not as drafting flaws.
